News feed (Instagram / Quora / ShareChat)

This page teaches you to design a home feed for 500 million users. The feed must load in under 200 milliseconds. You will also learn to answer the two hard follow-ups: celebrities and "billions of users".

Time-box: 45–60 min Seen: ~8 reports, 2024–26 Probes: fan-out, celebrity problem, cursor pagination, schema
PhonePeFlipkartShareChatFreshworksGoldman SachsIntuit
In 30 seconds
  • When someone posts, copy the post ID into each follower's feed list in Redis. This is called "fan-out on write".
  • Celebrities are different. Their posts are fetched when a follower opens the app. Mixing both ways is "hybrid fan-out".
  • Load the next page with a bookmark of (createdAt, postId), called a cursor. Never use a page number.
  • The feed cache is only a copy. Posts and the follow list are the real data.
Picture it

A newspaper vendor drops the paper at each subscriber's door every morning. That is "push". A paper with 10 crore readers cannot do this. Its readers pick up a copy at the stall instead. That is "pull". Your feed does both. Small authors get doorstep delivery. Celebrities use stall pickup.

Words you'll see
Fan-out
Copying one new post into the feeds of many followers. One post "fans out" to many places.
Push and pull
Push means we copy the post into followers' feeds when it is written. Pull means we collect posts only when someone opens their feed.
Celebrity
An account with a huge number of followers. Here, more than 100,000 followers.
Redis sorted set
A list in Redis, an in-memory store, that keeps items in order by a number called the score. We use the post time as the score.
Cursor
A bookmark that says "give me posts older than this one". It replaces page numbers.
Hydrate
Turn a bare post ID into the full post: text, photo, author name and like count.
Kafka
A message queue. Services drop events into it, and workers read them later at their own pace.
CDN
Content delivery network. Servers close to users that keep copies of images and videos, so they load fast.

01What the interviewer is grading

Fan-out with numbers

Choose push, pull or hybrid. Back it with numbers: how followers are spread, and how many reads happen per write.

Celebrity problem

One celebrity post can mean millions of writes and one overloaded server. Fix it with a clear follower limit.

Clean schema

Pick the right partition keys. Store posts by author. Store each follow twice, once in each direction.

Stable pagination

Use cursors, not page numbers. Then new posts do not shift what is on page 2.

Feed is derived

The feed cache can be rebuilt any time. Posts and the follow list are the real data.

Media path

Upload photos straight to file storage and serve them from a CDN. They should never pass through app servers.

Go deeper: what each company actually asked

The reports fall into two styles. The first style is schema-heavy. Flipkart asked for an Instagram-style feed where influencers promote brands. Users follow brands or influencers. Posts have likes, comments and sticker reactions. When you unfollow someone, their posts must leave your feed. The interview then went deep into LLD (low-level class design) and the database schema.

The second style is scale-heavy. PhonePe asked "Design Instagram". The interviewer refused a design with one queue message per follower. Their reason was that it does not scale to billions of users. Freshworks asked about sharding and the celebrity problem. They also asked what happens when the number of servers changes. Goldman Sachs started with a Twitter LLD and moved into HLD fan-out. ShareChat asked for a paged feed API using timestamp cursors. PhonePe's internal Quora version asked about database choice, search, login and how microservices talk to each other.

02Clarify and scope

Functional

  • Create posts with photos or video. Follow and unfollow users, brands and influencers.
  • A home feed of followed accounts. Newest first or ranked. Endless scrolling.
  • Like, comment and react. Show counts on each post.
  • A profile feed that shows one author's posts.

Non-functional

  • 500 million daily active users (DAU). 99 out of 100 feed loads finish in under 200 ms (p99).
  • Followers see a new post within about 5 seconds. A short delay is fine (eventual consistency).
  • Reads must always work, even during failures (AP). Posts are never lost.
  • Read-heavy: about 100 feed reads for every post written.

Out of scope

  • Stories, reels, direct messages and the ads auction.
  • The ranking model itself. Design the plumbing, not the machine learning.

04API design

  1. UploadThe app first asks for a short-lived upload link. It then sends the photo straight to file storage (S3) with that link, not through our servers. This link is called a presigned URL. Endpoint: POST /v1/media/upload-url.
  2. PostThe app creates the post and attaches a unique request ID. If the network fails and the app sends it again, the post is still created only once. Endpoint: POST /v1/posts with an Idempotency-Key header.
  3. FeedThe app asks for 20 feed items. The answer also holds a bookmark for where to continue, and says whether more items exist. Endpoint: GET /v1/feed?limit=20&cursor=…, returning nextCursor and hasMore.
  4. BannerThe app asks how many new posts arrived since it last looked. That number fills the "new posts" banner. Endpoint: GET /v1/feed/new-count?since=….

05Data model

post

Keep each author's posts together, newest first. Showing a profile is then one read from one place. (Partition by author_id, sort by the time-ordered Snowflake post_id.)

follow + follower

Each follow is stored twice, so both questions are one fast read. One copy answers "whom do I follow" (keyed by follower_id). The other answers "who follows me", which fan-out needs (keyed by followee_id).

feed:{userId}

Each active user's ready-made feed: a list of up to 500 post IDs, kept in time order. It is a Redis sorted set, scored by post time in milliseconds.

recent:{authorId}

Each author's last ~200 post IDs. When you open your feed, celebrity posts are read from here instead of being copied to you.

Detail that impresses

Redis stores sorted-set scores as decimal numbers (doubles). A double holds whole numbers exactly only up to 253. A 64-bit Snowflake ID is bigger, so it would lose digits. So use the time in milliseconds as the score and the post ID as the item. When two posts share a time, the cursor uses the post ID to break the tie.

Posts, reactions and comments are mostly added, rarely changed. They are read by one partition key. Cassandra suits that well. Instagram itself started on sharded Postgres. The feed lives in Redis because it is hot, small and can be rebuilt.

Post service

Checks the post and saves it. In the same save, it writes a "post created" note that is later sent to Kafka, so the event is never lost (an outbox row). Then it replies. Copying to feeds happens later, so the user never waits for it.

Fan-out workers

If the author has over 100k followers, the worker only updates the author's recent-posts list (recent:{author}). Otherwise it reads the followers page by page and keeps the active ones. It adds the post to their feeds in batches, one batch per Redis server, and trims each feed (ZADD plus trim).

Feed service

Combines the pushed feed with celebrity posts by time. Removes blocked, deleted and unfollowed. Can rank. Then fills in post details in one batch read.

Counters

Likes go through Kafka and are added up in batches. The live count sits in Redis. A viral post's count is split across several keys.

Go deeper: every component
  • Post service. It checks the post, saves it with an outbox row, and replies. An outbox row is saved in the same transaction as the post and sent to Kafka later, so the event is never lost. The service does not fan out while the user waits.
  • Fan-out workers. They read post.created events. If the author is a celebrity (say over 100k followers), they do nothing more. That post goes only into recent:{author}. Otherwise they page through the author's followers and keep only active users. Then they send ZADD plus ZREMRANGEBYRANK into each follower's feed. Commands are batched (pipelined) and grouped by Redis server.
  • Feed service. It reads the user's pushed feed. It fetches recent posts from the celebrities the user follows. That list is usually small and cached. It merges everything by time. It removes blocked, deleted and unfollowed posts. It can rank the result. Then it fills in post bodies, author cards and counts from caches, using one batch read (multi-get).
  • Graph service. It owns follow links in both directions. It publishes an event on every follow and unfollow.
  • Counters. Likes go to Kafka and are added up in batches. The live count lives in Redis. A viral post's counter is split across several keys, so no single key gets overloaded.
  • Search (Quora version). Elasticsearch, kept in sync by CDC (change data capture: reading every change from the post store's log).
  • Media path. The app uploads with a presigned link to S3. A media pipeline then resizes and converts it. The CDN serves the result. Kafka carries one event per post, not one per follower.

08Design choices

How posts reach feeds

Push (fan-out on write)

  • Reading is cheap. The feed is one ready-made list, so the cost stays flat (O(1)).
  • One write per follower. A celebrity post means 100M inserts and minutes of delay.
  • Wasted writes for users who never open the app

Most authors, with active followers.

Pull (fan-out on read)

  • Writing is cheap, one save per post (O(1)). The feed is always fresh.
  • Each read collects posts from up to thousands of accounts, 150k times a second

Celebrities, and small internal apps.

Hybrid usual pick

  • Both sides stay small: one list plus a few celebrity reads
  • Merge code and a follower limit to tune

The default at scale. Twitter ended up here.

What the feed stores

Post IDs usual pick

  • Small cache. Edits and deletes show up everywhere.
  • An extra step to fill in details on read

Always.

Full posts

  • One read
  • Huge cache. Old copies stay after an edit.

Never at this scale.

Post store

Cassandra

  • Handles many writes, many data centres, and reads by partition key
  • No joins. You design a table per query.

Very large scale.

Sharded Postgres either works

  • Familiar, with transactions and constraints
  • Moving data when you add shards takes work

Up to large scale. Instagram did it.

Go deeper: why hybrid wins

Hybrid is the expected answer. Large systems end up there. Twitter's early design pushed every tweet into each follower's in-memory timeline. They found celebrity tweets took minutes to reach the slowest followers (p99). The fix was to merge high-follower accounts in at read time.

09Deep dives

9.1 "A message queue doesn't scale to billions" (PhonePe)

Rejected: 1 message per follower

  • One celebrity post becomes 100M messages
  • The queue becomes the slow point and the backlog

Defensible: 1 event per post say this

  • About 5k events per second at the peak. Easy for Kafka.
  • Clear rules limit how many copies we make
  • The pull path keeps feeds correct even without the queue
Go deeper: the full answer

The interviewer rejected one picture: "for each follower, put a message in the queue, and workers write it into feeds". That design puts all the copying inside the queue. One celebrity post becomes 100M messages. The queue becomes the slow point and the backlog. Here is an answer you can defend.

  • The queue carries one event per post. That is about 5k per second at the peak. It is never one per follower. Kafka handles that easily.
  • Rules limit the copying. No push for celebrities, who are pulled at read time. No push for inactive users, whose feed is rebuilt when they next log in. Each pushed list is capped at 500.
  • Writes are batched by Redis server. A worker groups follower IDs by Redis slot and sends them together. So 10,000 followers cost a few dozen network trips, not 10,000.
  • The pull path is a full citizen. The feed service can build any user's feed from recent:{author} lists. It does this if pushing falls behind or the cache is empty. So the queue only makes feeds faster. Correctness does not depend on it.
  • Work grows with active readers, not total users. "Billions of users" is fine. Most of them are not reading right now, and we do no write work for them.

9.2 Celebrity problem and sharding (Freshworks)

Hot spotFix
Writes: 100M feed insertsHybrid fan-out. Mark celebrities with hysteresis: become one at 100k followers, stop being one at 80k.
Reads: 50M users hit one recent:{author}Keep 8 copies of the list and read a random one (recent:{author}:{0..7}). Add a tiny cache inside each server that keeps it for 1–5 s (Caffeine, TTL).
Storage: huge follower partitionSplit one celebrity's follower list into smaller buckets ((followee_id, bucket))
Adding serversConsistent hashing or Redis Cluster's 16,384 slots, so only ~1/N of keys move. Split by user ID, not by post time.
Go deeper: details and sharding vs partitioning
  • Write side. Hybrid fan-out covers it. Mark accounts as celebrities by follower count, with hysteresis. They enter at 100k followers and leave at 80k. Then an account near the line does not flip back and forth on every follow.
  • Hot keys on the read side. 50M users may fetch one celebrity's recent:{author} or post body. Copy that key to several Redis replicas, or keep copies like recent:{author}:{0..7}. Also keep a small cache inside the feed service (Caffeine, with a 1–5 s TTL, the time before it expires).
  • Hot storage partition. A celebrity's follower list is too wide for one Cassandra partition. Split it into buckets as (followee_id, bucket).
  • Changing the number of servers. Use consistent hashing with virtual nodes, or Redis Cluster's 16,384 hash slots. Then adding a server moves only about 1/N of the keys, not everything. Split by user ID, not by post time. Otherwise the newest shard takes all the writes.
  • Sharding vs partitioning (they asked). Partitioning splits a table into parts, maybe on one server. Sharding is partitioning across machines. It adds the work of routing requests and rebalancing data.

9.3 Cursor pagination (ShareChat)

Offset

  • New posts push older items down, so page 2 repeats page 1
  • Deep pages are slow

Keyset cursor usual pick

  • Stays correct when new posts arrive, and is fast
  • The bookmark holds the last post's time and ID. The ID breaks ties when two posts share a time ((created_at, post_id)).
  • You cannot jump to page N

All feeds.

-- Profile feed or pull path from the post store
SELECT post_id, author_id, created_at FROM post
 WHERE author_id = :authorId
   AND (created_at, post_id) < (:cursorTs, :cursorId)
 ORDER BY created_at DESC, post_id DESC
 LIMIT 20;

-- Home feed from Redis: scores are timestamps, exclusive upper bound
ZRANGE feed:{u42} 1728290000000 -inf BYSCORE REV LIMIT 0 30
-- inclusive bound: at the boundary timestamp, drop ids >= cursorId in code,
-- then keep 20; a 21st surviving item means hasMore

Pull-to-refresh uses the top item's cursor in the other direction: "newer than this". Snowflake IDs already sort by time. So post-store queries can use the post ID alone.

Go deeper: why timestamp-only cursors fail

Page numbers (offsets) break a live feed. New posts push items down, so page 2 repeats items from page 1. Deep offsets are also slow. Use a keyset cursor of (created_at, post_id) instead. Two posts can share the same timestamp. The post ID breaks that tie. A cursor with only the timestamp gets this wrong.

9.4 Unfollow, delete and the Flipkart schema

Go deeper: the full Flipkart follow-ups
  • Unfollow must remove their posts. The pushed feed holds IDs from many authors. Scanning 500 entries is cheap. On unfollow, the feed service hides that author right away at read time, using the cached follow list. A background job later removes the IDs from the sorted set. It is correct at once and cleaned up later.
  • Delete a post. Mark it deleted in the post store. The fill-in step drops it. Fan-out workers do not need to visit every feed.
  • Brands vs influencers. Use one account table with a type, and one follow table. Everything becomes "follow an account". Endorsements are a join table between posts and brands. So a brand page can show influencer posts about it.
  • Sticker reactions. A reaction table with a type. Counts are added up per (post, type).

Go deeper: full notes, plus PhonePe's internal Quora
  • What breaks first at 10x. Fan-out falls behind at busy times, like a festive sale or a cricket final. Kafka soaks up the burst. Feeds run a few seconds late, which is acceptable. When the delay grows, lower the celebrity limit on the fly. Then we pull more and push less.
  • Losing Redis. Say a feed server dies. The affected users get their feed rebuilt by pull, from recent:{author} and the post store. Then Redis is refilled. Slower, not broken. Never treat Redis as the main copy of the data.
  • Safe repeats. If Kafka delivers an event again, the same post ID goes into the sorted set again. ZADD keeps one copy per item, so duplicates are harmless. This property is called idempotent.
  • Ordering. Feeds sort by post time, not arrival time. So fan-out arriving out of order is invisible.
  • Consistency. Feeds choose availability over strict consistency (AP). They catch up within seconds (eventually consistent). But authors must see their own post at once. This is called read-your-own-writes. So the feed service adds the viewer's own recent posts to the response.
  • Many regions. Each user has a home region. Posts are copied to every region (Cassandra multi-DC). Fan-out runs in each follower's home region, from the copied event.
  • Ranking at scale. First gather about 500 candidates: the pushed feed, celebrity posts and recommended posts. A light model scores them all. A heavier model re-ranks the top 50. Cache each user's ranked page for a minute.
  • Internal Quora (PhonePe). Use Postgres for questions, answers and votes. The data is strongly related and the scale is moderate. Use Elasticsearch for search, kept in sync by CDC. Log in through the company's single sign-on (SSO, using OIDC). Check the JWT at the gateway, with roles for moderators. Services call each other with REST or gRPC for queries. Kafka events drive notifications, search indexing and reputation scores. A feed of "questions in topics I follow" is the same hybrid model at a much smaller scale. Pull alone is fine for an internal tool.

11Mistakes that got candidates rejected

Rejected for this
  • Pure push with one message per follower, and then no answer for celebrities or "billions of users".
  • Pure pull with a SQL IN (followees) query sorted by time, at 150k queries per second.
  • Page-number pagination, or a timestamp-only cursor with nothing to break ties.
  • Treating the Redis feed as the real data, with no way to rebuild it.
  • Storing full post objects in every follower's feed.
  • A schema with no thought about partition keys. For example, one follow table that answers neither "whom do I follow" nor "who follows me" quickly.
  • Sending photo bytes through app servers instead of presigned uploads and a CDN.

13Quick check

An account with 80M followers posts. What does the fan-out worker do?

This account is above the celebrity limit. So its posts are fetched and merged when followers read. Pushing would cost 80M writes and minutes of delay.

Why does the feed use a (createdAt, postId) cursor instead of ?page=2?

A cursor says "older than this item". That stays correct as new posts arrive. A timestamp alone can skip or repeat posts made in the same millisecond.

A Redis feed server is lost. What happens to its users?

The feed is only a copy. Posts and the follow list are the real data. So the pull path can rebuild the feed. Slower, not broken.

Why use the timestamp, not the Snowflake post ID, as the sorted-set score?

Redis scores are decimal numbers that lose digits above 2^53. So use the time in ms as the score and the post ID as the item. The cursor uses the item to break ties.

14Interview questions

Your queue-based fan-out won't scale to a billion users. What now?

Send one Kafka event per post, not one per follower. Push only to active followers of normal authors. Batch those writes per Redis server. Pull celebrity posts at read time. Build feeds for inactive users only when they return. Now work grows with posts and active readers. The pull path also means the queue only speeds things up. Feeds stay correct without it.

What is the celebrity problem?

An account with 100M followers posts. With push, that costs 100M feed writes. This causes delay and overloaded partitions. Millions of reads also hit the same keys. Fix it with hybrid fan-out, where celebrities are pulled. Add copies of hot keys and small local caches. Split huge follower partitions into buckets.

How do you design the paginated feed API?

Use GET /feed?limit=20&cursor=.... The cursor is opaque and holds (createdAt, postId). Query for posts "older than the cursor", sorted by time and then ID. Offsets repeat items as new posts arrive, and get slower on deep pages. Return nextCursor and hasMore.

A user unfollows someone. Their posts must vanish now.

At read time, filter against the cached follow list. Then the very next request is correct. In the background, remove that author's IDs from the user's sorted set. Do not make the unfollow wait for the feed cleanup.

What happens when you add servers to the feed cache?

With consistent hashing or Redis Cluster slots, only about 1/N of keys move. The moved feeds start empty (cold). The feed service falls back to pull and refills them. Move slots a few at a time, so you avoid a sudden wave of empty caches.

How do like counts work for a viral post?

Likes go to Kafka and are added up in short time windows. The count lives in Redis, split across several sub-keys. A read sums the sub-keys. The total is saved to the database now and then. Live counts are slightly approximate, and users accept that.

Twitter LLD to HLD: what changes?

In LLD, a TimelineService merges posts from followed users using a heap. In HLD, that same merge becomes the pull path for celebrities. Everything else is prepared ahead by fan-out workers into per-user caches. A heap merge over 2,000 followed accounts at 150k reads per second is too expensive.

How do media uploads work without hurting app servers?

The app asks for a presigned URL and uploads straight to S3. The post stores the media ID. An event starts resizing and video conversion into several sizes. The CDN serves them. App servers never handle the bytes.

15Cheat sheet

Cheat sheet
  • 500M daily users. About 150k feed reads per second at peak, about 5k posts per second. Pushing everything costs 230k writes per second.
  • Hybrid fan-out. Push to active followers. Pull celebrities (over 100k followers, with hysteresis) at read time.
  • Kafka carries one event per post, never one per follower.
  • The feed is a Redis sorted set feed:{user}. Score is the time in ms, item is the post ID, kept at 500.
  • Store IDs and fill in details with a batch read. The cache can be rebuilt. It is never the real data.
  • Keyset cursor (createdAt, postId), sent as opaque base64.
  • Store follows in both directions. Split celebrity follower partitions into buckets.
  • Unfollow and delete: hide at read time now, clean up in the background.
  • Media: presigned upload to S3, served by the CDN.
