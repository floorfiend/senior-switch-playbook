RAG fundamentals, end to end

RAG (retrieval-augmented generation) means: first find the right pages, then let the AI answer from them. You will build the whole pipeline in about 100 lines of Python that run offline. The steps are load, chunk, embed, store, retrieve, assemble and generate. You will also learn to explain every choice in it.

Time: 4 hours Stack: Python + numpy (offline), Voyage AI embeddings, Claude Asked at: 5 companies in the reports
AdobeMastercardTeradataAvaamoEquinix
In 30 seconds
  • RAG is a search engine whose results are pasted into the AI's prompt.
  • Ahead of time, you cut documents into pieces and turn each piece into numbers. For each question, you find the best few pieces and then ask the AI.
  • For contracts, make one piece per clause. Keep the clause number with it.
  • Is all your knowledge under about 200,000 tokens (about 500 pages)? Then skip RAG. Put everything in the prompt and cache it.
Picture it

Think of an open-book exam. Before the exam, a librarian builds an index of the book by topic. This is called ingestion. For each question, you flip to the 3–5 most useful pages. This is retrieval. You answer only from those pages, and you write down the page numbers. These are citations.

Words you'll see
LLM
Large language model. The AI that writes the answer, like Claude. It only knows what it was trained on, plus what you put in the prompt.
Token
A small piece of text the AI reads, about three quarters of a word. Prices and limits are counted in tokens.
Chunk
One small piece of a document, like one clause of a contract. We search chunks, not whole files.
Embedding
A list of numbers that stands for the meaning of a text. Texts with similar meaning get similar numbers, like nearby pins on a map.
Vector
The list of numbers itself, for example 1,024 numbers. Think of it as an arrow pointing in some direction.
Cosine similarity
A score for how much two arrows point the same way. 1 means the same direction (same meaning). Near 0 means unrelated.
Top-k
The k best matches. With k = 3, you keep the three closest chunks.
Tenant
One customer company in a shared system. Tenant A must never see tenant B's contracts.

Go deeper: why RAG exists

Claude knows nothing about your customers' contracts. You have three options. Option one: paste everything into the prompt. This works until the documents are too many or too costly to send. Option two: fine-tune, which means extra training of the model. That teaches style, not facts, and it is out of date the next day. Option three: retrieve the few useful passages for each question and paste only those.

RAG is the third option. Think of an Elasticsearch query. Its results go into the prompt instead of onto a results page.

First question to ask: do you need RAG at all? Anthropic gives this advice in its contextual retrieval write-up. If your knowledge base is under about 200,000 tokens (roughly 500 pages), put all of it in the prompt. Then use prompt caching, so you don't pay full price every time. One 80-page master services agreement (MSA) does not need a vector database. Contract libraries for ten thousand tenants do.

04Embeddings and similarity

Meaning, not words

"Liability cap" and "aggregate liability shall not exceed" share few words. Yet their embeddings sit close together.

Weak on exact ids

Exact codes like "clause 12.2" or "C-1042" often match worse than with keyword search. That is why hybrid search exists.

Go deeper: embeddings and model choice in full

An embedding model turns text into a vector, for example 1,024 numbers. It does this so that texts with similar meaning point in similar directions. Picture pins on a map: similar ideas land in the same neighbourhood. "Liability cap" and "aggregate liability shall not exceed" share almost no words. Yet their vectors end up close together. That is the advantage over keyword search.

The cost is this: exact codes like "clause 12.2" or "C-1042" are often matched worse than with keyword search. That is why hybrid search exists.

Cosine similarity measures the angle between two vectors: cos(a, b) = (a · b) / (|a| |b|). It goes from −1 to 1, and higher means more similar. Voyage embeddings are normalised, which means each vector has length 1. Then cosine similarity equals the dot product, and Euclidean distance gives the same ranking. So the choice of metric matters less than people think. But you must use the metric the model was trained for. You must also build your index with the matching operator class.

Anthropic does not offer an embedding model. Its docs point to Voyage AI. The current Voyage text models are voyage-4-large (best quality), voyage-4 (balanced) and voyage-4-lite (cheapest and fastest). Each has a 32k-token context and 1,024 dimensions by default. 256, 512 and 2048 dimensions are also available. There is also voyage-law-2, made for legal text (16k context, 1,024 dimensions). It is the natural one to test on contracts. What matters in practice:

  • Same model for documents and questions. Vectors from different models cannot be compared. Changing model means re-embedding all documents, so store embedding_model as a column.
  • input_type. Pass "document" when you store and "query" when you search. Voyage adds a different hidden instruction for each, which improves search. Never leave it out.
  • Dimensions vs cost. 1,024 float32 numbers take 4 KB per chunk. 10 million chunks take about 40 GB, before the index. Fewer dimensions, or quantisation (storing smaller numbers), save a lot of memory. You lose a little recall.
  • Language and domain. Indian contracts mix English with Hindi terms and references to laws. Test on your golden set (a list of questions with known right answers), not on a public leaderboard.

05Chunking and top-k

Fixed-size

  • Simple and predictable.
  • Cuts clauses in the middle of a sentence.

Recursive

  • Tries paragraph, then line, then sentence, then character.
  • A good default for normal prose.

Semantic

  • Splits where the topic changes.
  • Costly and hard to debug.

Structure-aware contracts

  • One clause per chunk.
  • Clause number and section saved as labels.

Start here

Prose: 300–800 tokens with 10–20% overlap. Contracts: one clause per chunk, no overlap. Then measure recall@k (how often the right chunk is in the top k).

Top-k

Use k = 3–8 directly. Or fetch 20–50 and rerank down to 5. Too small misses clause 12.2. Too big adds cost and noise.

Score threshold

Set a minimum score. Then off-topic questions find nothing, and the AI says "not in the documents".

Go deeper: strategies, size, overlap and top-k in full

Fixed-size

Cut every N tokens, repeating M tokens of overlap. Simple and predictable. But it cuts clauses mid-sentence. "Shall not exceed" lands in one chunk and the amount in the next.

Recursive

Split on paragraphs, then lines, then sentences, then characters, until each chunk fits. A good default for prose. LangChain calls it RecursiveCharacterTextSplitter. Spring AI's simpler TokenTextSplitter counts tokens and defaults to 800.

Semantic

Embed each sentence. Start a new chunk when a sentence is much less similar to the one before. Topic edges are better, but ingestion costs more and is harder to debug.

Structure-aware

Split on the document's own structure: headings, numbered clauses, table rows. For contracts this clearly wins. One clause per chunk, with clause number and section heading as labels.

A good starting point for prose is 300–800 tokens with 10–20% overlap. Overlap means the end of one chunk is repeated at the start of the next. It patches bad cuts at the edges. For contracts, use one clause per chunk with no overlap. Splitting by structure avoids most bad cuts, so overlap is rarely needed. Then measure recall@k on a golden set and adjust. Chunk size is a setting you tune by testing, not a belief.

k is how many chunks you give the AI. If k is too small, you miss the exception in 12.2 that changes the answer. If k is too large, you pay for more tokens and wait longer. The answer also gets buried in noise. Researchers call this "lost in the middle". Typical values: k = 3–8 straight from the vector search. Or fetch 20–50 and rerank down to 5. Also add a minimum similarity score. Then an off-topic question finds nothing. The AI says "not in the documents" instead of answering from loosely related clauses.

Step 2: structure-aware chunking for contracts

A regular expression finds clause headings like 12, 12.1 and 12.1.3. A heading with no text under it becomes a label, not a chunk.

Show clause_splitter.py
import re

# A clause heading: line start, number like 12 or 12.1 or 12.1.3, then a title or text.
HEADING = re.compile(r"^(?P<no>\d+(?:\.\d+)*)\.?\s+(?P<title>[^\n]+)$", re.MULTILINE)


def split_clauses(doc_id: str, text: str, max_chars: int = 1500) -> list[dict]:
    matches = list(HEADING.finditer(text))
    chunks = []
    for i, m in enumerate(matches):
        end = matches[i + 1].start() if i + 1 < len(matches) else len(text)
        body = text[m.start():end].strip()
        if "\n" not in body and "." not in m.group("no"):
            continue  # bare section heading like "12. Limitation of Liability": kept as metadata only
        top = m.group("no").split(".")[0]
        parent = next((x.group("title") for x in matches if x.group("no") == top), "")
        # ponytail: oversize clauses are cut on blank lines only; add sentence split if clauses run long
        parts = [body] if len(body) <= max_chars else [p for p in body.split("\n\n") if p.strip()]
        for j, part in enumerate(parts):
            chunks.append({
                "id": f"{doc_id}#{m.group('no')}" + (f"-{j}" if len(parts) > 1 else ""),
                "clause_no": m.group("no"),
                "section": parent,
                "text": part,
            })
    return chunks

Step 3: the whole pipeline, offline

fake_embed counts words into 256 buckets using a hash. It shows the mechanics, not real meaning. In the next lesson, pgvector replaces the simple list store.

Show toy_rag.py
"""Offline RAG pipeline: load -> chunk -> embed -> store -> retrieve -> assemble -> generate."""
import hashlib
import os
import re

import numpy as np

from clause_splitter import MSA, split_clauses

DIM = 256


def fake_embed(text: str) -> np.ndarray:
    # ponytail: hashed bag-of-words stands in for a real model; swap for Voyage in production
    v = np.zeros(DIM)
    for word in re.findall(r"[a-z]+", text.lower()):
        v[int(hashlib.md5(word.encode()).hexdigest(), 16) % DIM] += 1.0
    n = np.linalg.norm(v)
    return v / n if n else v


class InMemoryStore:
    def __init__(self):
        self.vectors, self.chunks = [], []

    def add(self, chunks):
        for c in chunks:
            self.vectors.append(fake_embed(c["section"] + " " + c["text"]))
            self.chunks.append(c)

    def search(self, query: str, k: int = 3, min_score: float = 0.1):
        q = fake_embed(query)
        scores = np.array(self.vectors) @ q  # unit vectors: dot product == cosine similarity
        top = np.argsort(-scores)[:k]
        return [(float(scores[i]), self.chunks[i]) for i in top if scores[i] >= min_score]


def assemble_prompt(question: str, hits) -> str:
    docs = "\n".join(
        f'<source id="{c["id"]}" clause="{c["clause_no"]}">\n{c["text"]}\n</source>' for _, c in hits
    )
    return (
        f"<sources>\n{docs}\n</sources>\n\n"
        f"<question>{question}</question>\n\n"
        "Answer only from the sources. Cite every claim as [source id]. "
        "If the sources do not contain the answer, say so."
    )


def generate(prompt: str) -> str:
    import anthropic

    resp = anthropic.Anthropic().messages.create(
        model="claude-haiku-4-5-20251001",
        max_tokens=512,
        system="You answer questions about contracts for a legal team.",
        messages=[{"role": "user", "content": prompt}],
    )
    return resp.content[0].text


if __name__ == "__main__":
    store = InMemoryStore()
    store.add(split_clauses("MSA-acme", MSA))  # load + chunk + embed + store
    question = "What is the liability cap and are there exceptions?"
    hits = store.search(question, k=3)  # retrieve
    for score, c in hits:
        print(f"{score:.3f}  {c['id']}")
    prompt = assemble_prompt(question, hits)  # assemble
    print(prompt)
    if os.environ.get("ANTHROPIC_API_KEY"):
        print(generate(prompt))  # generate

Go deeper: steps 2–4 explained in full

Contracts number their clauses, so use that numbering. The regular expression matches headings like 12, 12.1 or 12.1.3 at the start of a line. Each match runs until the next heading. A bare section heading, like "12. Limitation of Liability", is not stored as a chunk. It becomes the section label on its sub-clauses. A clause that is too long is split on blank lines instead.

Each chunk has a stable id (doc#clause). It is used for citations. It also makes re-loading idempotent: doing it twice has the same effect as doing it once, because you upsert on the id. Each chunk also carries labels (metadata) to filter and display. In production, also add tenant_id, contract_id, page, effective_date and embedding_model.

To run without any API, fake_embed hashes each word into one of 256 buckets and counts them. Then it scales the vector to length 1. It is a stand-in that shows the mechanics. Make the length 1, so the dot product equals cosine similarity. Then sort the scores and keep the top k. It does not understand meaning.

The in-memory store is just a list of vectors. pgvector replaces it in the next lesson. We embed the section heading together with the clause text. This is a small form of "contextual" chunk enrichment: it gives each chunk a bit of its surrounding context.

To use real embeddings, replace fake_embed with the calls below. Use input_type="document" in add and input_type="query" in search. During ingestion, send many texts per request (batching). Voyage vectors already have length 1, so the dot product in search stays correct.

Step 5: prompt assembly that makes citations work

XML with ids

Put each source in a tag with an id. Then a citation like [MSA-acme#12.2] can link to the PDF page.

Refusal path

Tell it: "If the sources do not contain the answer, say so." Otherwise it fills gaps from what it learned in training.

Sources first

Put the long sources at the top. Put the question last.

Validate citations

Every id the answer cites must be one you supplied.

User turn, not system

The other party in the deal wrote the retrieved text. Treat it as untrusted.

Go deeper: assembly rules and the generate step
  • Wrap each source in XML tags with an id. Claude handles XML-tagged text well. The id lets you require citations like [MSA-acme#12.2]. Your UI can turn them into links to the PDF page.
  • Give it a way to refuse. Say: "If the sources do not contain the answer, say so." Without this, the model fills gaps from its training data. In legal work that is a hallucination: a confident answer that is made up.
  • Put sources first and the question last. Long context goes above the instruction.
  • Check citations after the answer comes back. Every cited id must be one you supplied. Remove or flag answers that cite ids you did not give.
  • Put tenant data in the user message, not the system prompt. Retrieved text is untrusted, because the other party in the deal wrote it. It must not get the authority of system instructions.

07How this shows up in interviews

Explain RAG end to end. (Asked almost everywhere.)

There are two halves. Offline: load documents, chunk them and embed each chunk. Store the vector, text and metadata in a vector index. Online: embed the question with the same model. Retrieve the top-k nearest chunks, with tenant and metadata filters. Put them into the prompt with ids. Generate an answer that cites those ids, then check the citations. Each stage has its own ways to fail and its own metric.

How do you choose chunk size and overlap?

Start from the document's structure. For contracts, use one clause per chunk, with its section heading as metadata. For prose, use 300–800 tokens with 10–20% overlap. Then tune on a golden set: questions paired with the chunks that should come back. Measure recall@k and answer faithfulness (does the answer stick to the sources). Smaller chunks give sharper vectors but less context. Larger chunks give more context, blurrier matches and more tokens.

Cosine similarity vs dot product vs Euclidean?

Cosine compares only direction. Dot product also includes length (magnitude). On vectors scaled to length 1, cosine equals the dot product. Euclidean distance then gives the same ranking. Use the metric the embedding model was trained for. Build the index with the matching operator class, or the database will not use the index.

How do you pick k?

k must be big enough for answers with several parts, like a clause plus its exception. Token cost and dilution limit it from above. Dilution means useful text gets lost among weak matches. In practice: fetch 20–50 candidates cheaply and rerank to 3–8. Add a minimum score, so off-topic questions get no context.

How do you choose an embedding model?

Test candidates on my own labelled questions, not on a leaderboard. Consider the domain (voyage-law-2 for contracts) and the language mix. Consider dimensions times corpus size, because that sets the memory needed. Also weigh latency, price, and support for separate query and document input types. I store the model version on each row, because switching means re-embedding everything.

RAG vs fine-tuning vs long context?

Under about 200k tokens of knowledge: put it all in the prompt with prompt caching. For large or often-changing private data that needs citations and per-user access control: use RAG. Fine-tuning changes behaviour and format, not facts. It also ignores access control. You cannot "un-train" one tenant's contract.

How do you stop hallucinations in a RAG answer?

Fix retrieval quality first. If the right chunk is not in the prompt, nothing later helps. Then tell the model to answer only from the sources, and to say when it cannot. Require citations, and check that each cited id exists. Apply a minimum score. Run faithfulness checks in offline evals (automatic tests on saved questions). Structured citations make every claim easy to audit.

How do you enforce multi-tenant isolation in retrieval?

The tenant id comes from the JWT (the signed login token), never from the request. It goes inside the vector query as a WHERE filter, not as filtering in Java afterwards. Back it with Postgres row-level security or per-tenant partitions. Then a bug in one query cannot leak data across tenants.

A user says the bot missed an obvious clause. How do you debug?

Replay the question and log the retrieved ids and scores. Was the clause chunked correctly? Is it in the index and up to date? Where does it rank, and what does the question match instead? Was it filtered out? Only then look at the prompt and the answer. Most "AI bugs" in RAG turn out to be search or ingestion bugs.

08Quick check

All your knowledge is one 80-page MSA, well under 200k tokens. What do you build?

Anthropic's advice: under about 200k tokens, skip RAG. The whole document fits in the prompt, and caching keeps it cheap.

The answer about the liability cap misses the exception in 12.2. What is the most likely cause?

If the right chunk is not in the prompt, nothing later can help. Chunk by clause, and use a larger k plus a reranker.

Why pass input_type="query" when searching with Voyage?

Voyage treats questions and documents a little differently, which improves matching. Documents use "document" when stored. Mixing them up or leaving it out hurts recall.

Where must the tenant filter run?

Filtering afterwards can leak data or return too few rows. The database should never return another tenant's chunk at all.

10Cheat sheet

Cheat sheet
  • Offline: load, chunk, embed, store. Per question: embed the question, retrieve, assemble, generate.
  • Under about 200k tokens: no RAG. Put it all in the prompt and cache it.
  • Contracts: one clause per chunk, id doc#clause, section as metadata. Prose: 300–800 tokens, 10–20% overlap.
  • Same embedding model on both sides. input_type is document or query. Store the model name on each row.
  • Length-1 vectors: cosine equals dot product. pgvector operators: <=>, <#>, <->.
  • k = 3–8, or fetch 20–50 and rerank to 5. Add a minimum score.
  • Sources in XML with ids, question last. Say "tell me if it's not there". Check citations.
  • Tenant filter inside the vector query, plus row-level security (RLS).
