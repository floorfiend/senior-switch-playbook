Stock Price Change Alert System

Users set alerts like "tell me when RELIANCE goes above ₹3,000". There are 50 million of these alerts. Every time a price changes, the system must find the alerts that just came true. It must notify each user exactly once, within a few seconds.

Time-box: 45–60 min Seen: 5 Uber SDE-2 / Senior reports, 2025–26; Groww and Upstox variants
UberGrowwUpstoxZerodha-style brokersRobinhood
In 30 seconds
  • Do not check every alert on every price change. Instead, group alerts by stock and keep their target prices sorted.
  • When the price moves, you only pick the alerts at the front of the sorted list. You never scan the whole table.
  • Use the lowest and highest price in each short time window. Then sudden jumps and quick spikes still trigger alerts.
  • Save each triggered alert once in the database. The hardest part is sending millions of notifications, not matching.
Picture it

Think of a railway platform where passengers stand in order of their stop. When the train reaches Dadar, everyone up to Dadar gets off together. Nobody checks every passenger at every station.

Words you'll see
Tick
One price update for a stock from the exchange. "RELIANCE traded at ₹3,004" is one tick.
Fire (an alert)
The alert's condition became true, so we notify the user.
Matcher
The service that compares new prices against stored alerts and decides which ones fire.
Kafka partition
Kafka is a message log. A partition is one ordered lane of it. Messages with the same key always go to the same lane, in order.
Offset commit
A reader telling Kafka "I have finished up to message N". After a crash, reading restarts from the last commit.
Idempotent
Doing the same thing twice has the same effect as doing it once. A repeated "save fire" must not create a second fire.
Fan-out
One event causing many outgoing messages. A market crash fires millions of alerts, so millions of pushes go out.
FCM / APNs
Firebase Cloud Messaging (Android) and Apple Push Notification service (iPhone). They deliver push notifications to phones.

01What the interviewer is grading

The matching insight

Checking 50M alerts against 50k price updates per second is impossible. Keep each stock's target prices sorted, so an update touches only the alerts it crosses.

Push vs pull

At each hand-off, decide who starts the conversation. Does the sender push data, or does the receiver ask for it? Explain why at each step.

Correct once

No duplicate alerts after a restart or when price wobbles around the target. No missed alerts when price jumps over the target.

Hot symbols and bursts

A popular stock like RELIANCE has millions of alerts. On a crash day, millions fire at the same moment.

Go deeper: what the reports said

An Uber SDE-2 candidate (Apr 2026) rated this round "Very Hard". They listed what was expected, word for word: push vs pull mechanism, DB design, API design, deep dive and trade-offs. In practice the grading splits into four things.

  • The matching insight. Do you check every alert on every tick? That fails at 50M alerts × 50k ticks per second. Or do you keep sorted target prices per stock, so a tick touches only the alerts it crosses?
  • Push vs pull at two boundaries. Market data to matcher is push (a live stream). Matcher to user is push too, by phone notification or WebSocket, not by the app asking again and again. Say why at each one.
  • Correct-once behaviour. No duplicate notifications when a matcher restarts. No duplicates when price flaps (moves up and down) around the target. No missed alerts when a tick jumps over the target.
  • Hot symbols and bursts. NIFTY or RELIANCE carry millions of alerts. A market-wide crash fires millions at once. Show where the system strains and how you protect the notification pipeline.

Related rounds test the same skills. Groww SDE-II asked for a stock order system. It must place buy, sell and stop-loss orders that arrive through a queue, within a 10 ms SLA (SLA means the promised response time). The feedback criticised missing multi-exchange support and scalability. Upstox SDE-2 asked for a trailing-loss system. A stop-loss is just a price alert whose action is "place order" instead of "send push".

02Clarify and scope

Functional

  • Create, list, edit and delete alerts (about 100 per user at most)
  • Fire when the condition becomes true, and notify by phone push and inside the app
  • Alert history ("fired at ₹3,001.40 at 10:31:02")

Non-functional

  • From price update to notification: 99 out of 100 within 2 seconds (p99 ≤ 2 s)
  • No duplicates and no misses (price jumps must still fire)
  • Up 99.95% of the time during market hours
  • Survive 10× normal load on crash days

Out of scope

  • Placing orders and risk checks (just mention where they would plug in)
  • Charts and live quote streaming
  • Building the exchange feed reader
Go deeper: six questions to ask first
  • Which alert types? Crossing a fixed price (above or below). A percentage move from yesterday's close. A percentage move within a time window. Trailing alerts. Each type needs a different index.
  • One-shot or recurring? One-shot alerts are deleted when they fire, which keeps the index small. Recurring alerts need re-arm rules. Without them, users get spammed when the price swings back and forth. This rule is called hysteresis.
  • Latency target? Retail apps accept "within 1–2 seconds of the price change". A stop-loss that places orders needs milliseconds (the Groww variant).
  • Delivery channels? Phone push (FCM or APNs), in-app WebSocket, SMS, email. Push is best-effort. You own the "fired" record. The phone owns showing it.
  • Price source? The exchange feed (NSE or BSE, through a data vendor) gives last traded price (LTP) ticks. Ask whether to alert on LTP or on bid and ask prices.
  • Market hours and halts? Ask about the pre-open session, circuit limits that pause trading, and corporate actions. A stock split changes every target price.

Go deeper: API rules, data model and each component
  • Take the user id from the JWT (the signed login token), never from the request body. Check the symbol against the list of real instruments. Reject a target that is already true at creation, like "RELIANCE above ₹2,000" when it trades at ₹2,950. Or offer to fire it straight away.
  • The Idempotency-Key header on create stops a double-tapped button from making two alerts. If-Match with a version number on PATCH stops two devices from overwriting each other's edits.
  • Send money as decimal strings, never floats, because floats round badly. Store it as NUMERIC(12,2), or as paise in a BIGINT.

Postgres fits here. Alert create, read, update and delete (CRUD) is small, relational and needs transactions. The heavy read pattern, by stock and by target price, is served from memory, not from the database. If the interviewer pushes for NoSQL, DynamoDB or Cassandra with partition key user_id works just as well. The matcher design does not change.

  • Feed handler turns the vendor feed into (symbol, ltp, exchange_ts, seq). It merges updates. If RELIANCE trades 40 times in 100 ms, matching only needs the lowest and highest price in that window. So it publishes (min, max, last) per stock every 100 ms.
  • Alert service is plain Spring Boot CRUD. It writes the alert and an outbox row in one transaction. Debezium streams the outbox to alert-changes. There is no dual write.
  • Matcher is a Kafka Streams or plain Kafka consumer app. Each instance owns some partitions and keeps a sorted index for each stock it owns. It reads both topics, so creates and ticks for one stock are handled by one thread, in order.
  • Fire recorder inserts into alert_events with a unique key. Then it changes the alert to FIRED, but only if it is still ACTIVE. This is the exactly-once point.
  • Notifier looks up device tokens and applies per-user rate limits. It bundles alerts into a digest ("5 of your alerts fired"). It calls FCM or APNs and retries on failure. The in-app path goes through a WebSocket gateway.

06Deep dives

6.1 Matching: flip the question around

The naive way checks 2.5M RELIANCE alerts × 5 ticks per second. That is 12.5M checks per second for one stock. Instead, keep target prices sorted and take from the front.

RELIANCE "above" index (₹, ascending)
Show Java SymbolIndex
final class SymbolIndex {                       // owned by one matcher thread: no locks
    private final TreeMap<Long, List<Long>> above = new TreeMap<>();   // paise → alertIds
    private final TreeMap<Long, List<Long>> below = new TreeMap<>(Comparator.reverseOrder());

    List<Long> onTick(long lo, long hi) {
        List<Long> fired = new ArrayList<>();
        drain(above.headMap(hi, true), fired);   // thresholds <= hi
        drain(below.headMap(lo, true), fired);   // thresholds >= lo (reverse order)
        return fired;
    }
    private static void drain(SortedMap<Long, List<Long>> hit, List<Long> out) {
        hit.values().forEach(out::addAll);
        hit.clear();                             // one-shot: removed from the index
    }
}
Go deeper: the full matching argument

The naive loop says: for every tick, for every alert on that stock, check the condition. With 2.5M alerts on RELIANCE and 5 ticks per second, that is 12.5M comparisons per second for one stock. Instead, keep two sorted lists per stock.

  • above: target prices sorted low to high. Say the window's highest price is hi. Fire every alert with a target at or below hi. In other words, take from the front until a target is above hi.
  • below: target prices sorted high to low. Fire every alert with a target at or above lo, the window's lowest price.

The cost is O(log n) to find the cut-off, plus O(k) for the k alerts that actually fire. You must do that O(k) work anyway. We use the window's lowest and highest price, not the last price. So a gap still fires, for example "opened at ₹3,020 after closing at ₹2,980". A spike that reverses within 100 ms also fires.

Percentage alerts fit into the same structure. Take "TCS moves more than 3% today", with yesterday's close at ₹4,000. That is two fixed prices: ₹4,120 above and ₹3,880 below. Calculate them when the market opens, or when the alert is created during the day. Recalculate them at the next day's open. Redis sorted sets are an acceptable alternative (ZRANGEBYSCORE alerts:RELIANCE:above -inf hi). They give you shared state instead of memory inside one process. You trade one network call per tick for easier recovery when a server fails.

6.2 Push vs pull, one hand-off at a time

  1. Exchange → systemPush (a live streaming feed). Asking every few seconds adds delay and misses spikes between asks.
  2. Ticks → matcherPush through Kafka, grouped by stock. Prices stay in order, can be replayed after a crash, and spread across many matchers.
  3. Alerts → matcherPush. A background tool copies each saved alert change into Kafka (CDC, change data capture). The matcher never queries the database while prices flow.
  4. Matcher → userPush (FCM or APNs, or WebSocket when the app is open). Polling 50M alerts every 5 s would be 10M requests per second saying "nothing changed".
  5. App → alert listPull. The app asks the server (a normal REST call, with a cache) only when the user opens the alerts screen.

One place where a batch pull wins is a daily-close alert. It can run once after the market closes. Mentioning it shows you choose per requirement.

6.3 Fire exactly once

"Kafka exactly-once"

  • For your side effects, Kafka is at-least-once. A message can be processed twice.
  • A crash before the offset commit means a replay, so a second push.
  • It says nothing about the FCM call.

A strong interviewer will ask what happens at FCM.

Exactly-once recording usual pick

  • A predictable id: alertId:fireSeq
  • INSERT … ON CONFLICT DO NOTHING and UPDATE … WHERE status='ACTIVE'
  • Use the same id as the push "collapse key". A duplicate push then replaces the old one instead of stacking.

Exactly-once delivery to a phone does not exist. This does.

Go deeper: the three layers of defence

Kafka gives at-least-once delivery. A matcher can fire an alert and crash before committing its offset. It restarts, replays the tick and fires again. So we add three layers of protection.

  1. Each fired event has an id built from its data: alertId + ":" + fireSeq. fireSeq goes up by one each time a recurring alert re-arms. So a recurring alert fires once per arming.
  2. The fire recorder runs INSERT INTO alert_events ... ON CONFLICT DO NOTHING. Then it runs UPDATE alerts SET status='FIRED', version=version+1 WHERE alert_id=? AND status='ACTIVE' AND fire_seq=?. Only the first attempt wins. The replay changes nothing.
  3. The notifier sends the push with the same id as a collapse key. Even a duplicate push then replaces the first one on the phone, instead of showing twice.

If you just say "Kafka exactly-once", a strong interviewer will ask what happens at the FCM call. Exactly-once delivery to a phone does not exist. Exactly-once recording, plus a display that ignores repeats, does.

6.4 Flapping, trailing stops, splits

Flapping

A price wobbling around ₹3,000 must not fire 40 times. Re-arm recurring alerts only after price moves 0.5% away (hysteresis) and a 15-min cooldown passes.

fire2,9993,0012,999≤ 2,985 → re-arm

Trailing stop

Trigger = peak × (1 − t). After a new high, all older alerts share that peak. So group them into cohorts by (trail %, peak) and update per group.

Corporate actions

A 1:2 stock split halves every target price. Adjust them before the market opens, and send the changes through alert-changes.

Go deeper: flapping and trailing stops in full
  • Flapping. A price swinging around ₹3,000 must not fire 40 times. One-shot alerts avoid this. Recurring alerts re-arm only after two things happen. Price must move back past a buffer band, say 0.5% below the target (hysteresis). And a cooldown must pass, say 15 min.
  • Trailing stop (Upstox). Trigger = highest price since creation × (1 − t). A per-alert peak cannot sit in a fixed sorted index. Here is the trick. Once the price makes a new high, every older alert on that stock shares the same peak. So group trailing alerts into cohorts by (trail %, peak). On a new high, merge all cohorts with a lower peak into one cohort at the new high, and re-key it. The index stays keyed by trigger price. Updates happen per cohort, not per alert.
  • Corporate actions. A 1:2 split halves every target price. Run an adjustment job before the market opens. Publish the updates through the same alert-changes topic.

6.5 The 10 ms variant (Groww stop-loss)

Retail alerts (≤ 2 s)

  • Kafka and a database write are in the path
  • Simple to replay and audit

Push notifications.

Stop-loss orders (≤ 10 ms)

  • LMAX-style: one thread per group of stocks, fed by multicast or shared memory
  • Order templates checked in advance; risk checks use cached balances
  • Saving to disk moves to a local journal, copied to other servers in the background

Mention multi-exchange (the same ISIN on NSE and BSE).

Go deeper: the low-latency path

Say the action is "place an order within 10 ms". Then Kafka and a database write on the busy path are too slow. Keep the same index, but run it LMAX-style. LMAX is a trading design where one thread owns the data and never waits for locks. Use one thread per group of stocks. Deliver the feed by UDP multicast or shared memory. Prepare and check order templates in advance. Write to a local append-only log and copy it to other servers in the background. Saving data moves off the critical path. The risk check (margin) runs on cached balances. Mention multi-exchange support. The same ISIN (the stock's global id) trades on NSE and BSE as separate symbols, with a mapping between them. The Groww feedback said exactly this was missing.

07Design choices

In-process TreeMap per symbol usual pick

  • Under a millisecond, no network call, no locks (one thread owns it)
  • Must rebuild after a failover; uses memory on each server

Default for retail alerts and stop-loss.

Redis ZSET per symbol + direction

  • Shared, and survives matcher restarts
  • A network call per tick; huge sorted sets on popular stocks; needs a Lua script to remove items atomically

Small team, moderate scale.

DB query per tick

  • Very easy to build
  • 50k queries per second on the main database; it falls over on crash days

Prototype only.

Flink / Kafka Streams state

  • Built-in checkpoints and exactly-once state
  • Heavy to operate; harder to split a popular stock

If the company already runs it.

Window min / max always

  • Gaps and spikes that reverse still fire

Last price only

  • Simple
  • Misses spikes that reverse inside the 100 ms merge window

Never.

08Scaling, failure handling and consistency

Hot symbols

Split one stock's alerts into sub-shards, RELIANCE#0..7, and send each tick to all of them. Ticks are cheap. Alerts are the volume.

Matcher failover

Rebuild the index from the compacted alert-changes topic or a standby copy. Then continue from the last committed offset.

Create vs tick order

Same key means same partition. So an alert created at .200 is never matched against an older .100 tick.

Crash-day burst

Add notifier workers reading alert-fired. Bundle alerts per user. Send stop-loss first. Slow down when FCM says "too many requests" (HTTP 429).

Stale feed

If a busy stock gets no tick for N seconds, raise an alarm and mark its price as stale.

Go deeper: full scaling notes
  • Hot symbols. Split a stock's alerts into N sub-shards (RELIANCE#0..7) across matchers. Send its ticks to all of them. Ticks are cheap. Alerts are the volume.
  • Matcher failover. The state can be rebuilt. When a matcher gets a partition, it loads the index from the compacted alert-changes topic. Another option is Kafka Streams with a RocksDB state store and a standby copy. Then it continues reading ticks from the last committed offset. Standby copies make failover take seconds instead of a full rebuild.
  • Order between "create" and "tick". Both topics use the stock symbol as key and have the same partition count. So the matcher sees them as one ordered stream per partition. An alert created at 10:31:00.200 is not matched against a 10:31:00.100 tick.
  • Crash-day burst. The alert-fired topic separates matching from sending. Add more notifier consumers and bundle alerts per user. Send in priority order: stop-loss actions and price-cross alerts before "3% move" alerts. FCM slows senders down. When it returns 429, back off and retry, and keep a token bucket (a simple rate limiter) per provider.
  • Stale feed. During market hours, a busy stock may get no tick for N seconds. Then raise an alarm and mark the data stale. Do not silently miss alerts.
  • Multi-region. Run matchers active-passive per region: one region works, one stands by. The feed comes from India anyway. Notifications can go out from any region. Alert CRUD stays on one primary database.

09Mistakes that got candidates rejected

Rejected for this
  • A cron job that runs SELECT * FROM alerts every few seconds and compares with the latest price. It does not scale, and it misses moves between runs.
  • Answering push vs pull with only "use WebSockets". The interviewer wanted the reasoning at each hand-off.
  • Matching only when price equals the target, or only on the last price. Then gaps and spikes are missed.
  • Ignoring duplicates from replays or flapping. Or claiming "Kafka exactly-once" covers the phone.
  • Never mentioning popular stocks or crash-day bursts. That is what "scalability" means here. The Groww feedback said: "needed more focus on scalability".

10Quick check

RELIANCE closes at ₹2,990 and opens next day at ₹3,010. Does the "above ₹3,000" alert fire?

We match on a range, so jumps over the target still fire. The fire record saves the real price, ₹3,010.

On a market-crash day, which part of the system is the slowest?

Matching happens in memory and costs O(log n + k). But millions of fires at once overload the push providers. So separate sending from matching, and bundle alerts per user.

A matcher crashes after firing an alert but before committing its offset. What stops a second push?

The replay tries to insert the same key. ON CONFLICT DO NOTHING turns that into no change. The collapse key handles any duplicate on the phone.

Why must price ticks and alert changes use the same partition key?

A shared key gives one ordered stream per stock. So a deleted alert cannot be fired by a later tick.

11Follow-up questions interviewers asked

Why not let the app poll for prices and check alerts on the phone?

Alerts must fire even when the app is closed, and then the phone is not running it. Also, 3M users polling every few seconds is millions of requests per second, mostly for nothing. Checking on the server and pushing to the phone is the only design that solves both.

A tick jumps from ₹2,990 to ₹3,010. Does the ₹3,000 alert fire?

Yes. The rule is "target ≤ window max", not "price == target". So jumps over the target still fire. The fire record stores the real price, ₹3,010, so the notification is honest.

How do you avoid firing an alert that was deleted a moment ago?

Deletes travel through the same symbol-keyed stream as ticks. So the matcher sees them in the right order. A fire might still race with a delete in the database. Then the fire recorder's conditional update (WHERE status='ACTIVE') fails, and the notification is dropped.

What happens when a matcher server dies during market hours?

Kafka hands its partitions to another matcher. The new owner restores the index from its standby state store or from the compacted alert-changes topic. Then it replays ticks from the last committed offset. Replayed fires are de-duplicated by alertId:fireSeq. Aim to recover in under 30 seconds. Replay with the window's min and max, so nothing that happened during the outage is missed.

How would you support "moves more than 2% in any 15-minute window"?

That is no longer a fixed price. Keep a sliding min and max over 15 minutes for each stock. A monotonic deque does this cheaply: a queue that keeps only values that could still become the min or max. Sort the alerts by percentage. Fire all alerts whose percentage ≤ (max − min)/min. The index is by percentage instead of price, but the idea is the same.

How do you test that no alert is missed?

Replay a recorded trading day through the matcher. Compare its output against a simple brute-force checker run offline. Also run the same comparison all the time in production, as a shadow job on a sample of stocks. Raise an alarm if the two disagree.

13Cheat sheet

Cheat sheet
  • Per stock, keep two TreeMaps: "above" sorted up and "below" sorted down. Take from the front on each tick window.
  • Merge ticks every 100 ms into (min, max, last). Match on min and max.
  • Turn percentage alerts into two fixed prices when the market opens.
  • Key ticks and alert changes by stock symbol. Each matcher then gets one ordered stream.
  • Use an outbox and Debezium, never a dual write. The matcher never queries the database.
  • Exactly-once means a unique alertId:fireSeq, a conditional status update and a push collapse key.
  • Popular stock: split into sub-shards and send ticks to all. Burst: bundle per user and prioritise.
  • 10 ms stop-loss: an LMAX-style single thread with a local journal.
